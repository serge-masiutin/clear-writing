# Presentation and illustrations

Use when presentation can be improved and that work is within the task.

## Start with what needs to be shown

State the point the reader needs to see. Then choose the medium. An illustration
explains or shows supporting evidence; a decorative picture merely takes up space.
Sometimes the best illustration is a short written example, with no image needed.

| What needs to be understood | Suitable form | What should be visible |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where a UI element is located | A screenshot with a pointer and caption | The relevant screen, element, and action |
| How to fill in a document | A form excerpt with an example | The field, format, and material condition |
| How options differ | A table | Shared parameters, units, and differences |
| The order of stages | A linear diagram | Stages, direction, and transitions |
| What to do under different conditions | A decision tree | Each branch's condition and outcome |
| Who interacts with whom | A relationship diagram | Participants and the meaning of each connection |
| How a product behaves | A demonstration or sequence of frames | The action and observable result |
| How a quantity changed | A chart | Axes, period, units, and source |
| Why a reply in a conversation does not work | A short dialogue | The remark, response, and needed change |

Do not automatically create visuals for every answer. Compare the effort of reading
them with that of a prose explanation. A table containing one simple row may be unnecessary.

## Guide the reader through the visual

Remove visual noise, highlight the relevant area, and label where to look and what
it means. Retain context without which the visual would mislead. A caption should
add understanding, not merely repeat the object's name.

In a diagram, distinguish physical arrangement, sequence, and causation. An unexplained
arrow might mean a transfer, dependency, or next step: show which one is intended.
Use a true scale for quantitative data; do not make a schematic look like a measurement.

A screenshot must match the interface version and state being described. Hide secrets
and personal information. Label a teaching mockup as a mockup; do not present an invented
image as evidence of a feature. Do not alter data in a screenshot to favor a conclusion.

For a video, state what it shows. The key action or result should be understandable
from the adjacent text, especially if the reader cannot play the video at that moment.

## Check at a glance

Visible headings, illustrations, and captions should let readers understand the material's
purpose and find the part they need. Formatting supports the structure of the meaning.
Do not obscure it with random bold words, callout boxes, and constant format changes.

Sequential material usually benefits from one reading flow. Place an image next to
the passage it explains without interrupting a connected idea. Follow the template
and formatting rules provided for the material. Editing the text does not itself
require redesigning it.

The number of lines depends on font and screen width. Do not turn a print book's
paragraph-length advice into a rigid rule for responsive pages or chat.

## The visual's message must also be honest

Compare the visual impression with the claim in the text. A chart using a favorable
period can imply growth even when nearby wording warns of risk. Do not hide a material
limitation in small print or make the “before” artificially worse to create a striking “after.”

Real materials are usually more useful than generic stock-photo people. Stock photography
is appropriate when the pictured subject is what you need and you have the right to use it.
Do not pass models off as team members, customers, or testimonial authors.

A list of work or overview of results can show volume when scale itself is the subject.
Do not overwhelm the reader with a list merely to impress, or substitute quantity
for evidence of quality.

## Worked example

Teaching task: explain why processing became faster. The only verified change is
the removal of one manual approval; there are no time measurements.

Show two diagrams with the same names for retained stages and highlight the removed
approval. Caption: “The process no longer includes a manual approval.” Do not draw
the second diagram at one-third the length and label it “Three times as fast”:
a change in the number of stages does not measure speed.
