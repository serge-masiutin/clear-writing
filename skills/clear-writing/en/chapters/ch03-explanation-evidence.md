# Explanation and evidence

Use when correct wording still does not give the reader a clear picture.

## From claim to application

Start with a situation the reader recognizes. Introduce only the necessary concepts,
then state the point. Show how it works in a specific case and explain what follows
for the task. If the reader already knows the context, go straight to the point.

Do not force every answer into one structure. For a new, complex topic, a possible
sequence is “familiar situation → concept → claim → application”; for a work question,
“answer → reason → material condition” may be enough.

## Example and counterexample

An example should clarify a choice or action. Supply only the circumstances needed
to understand it: who acts, in what situation, what they do, and what they get.
Do not add a character's biography when it changes nothing.

Prefer a real, verified case. If using an invented one, label it: “suppose” or
“teaching example.” Do not present it as a customer story, statistics, or a real
product or service feature. Do not turn a single example into the definition of an entire category.

A counterexample helps where a mistake is likely. Show a plausible misunderstanding,
why it does not fit, and what to do. Do not invent a foolish opponent to make the
comparison flattering. A counterexample may be unnecessary in a short answer.

If advice is correct but unhelpful, find the obstacle to following it. “Check the data”
becomes more useful when it specifies what to check, where to see it, and what to
decide if values differ. Do not add unknown commands or procedures.

## Give numbers a frame of reference

Check the unit, period, sample, and basis of comparison. Give an unfamiliar quantity
a meaningful scale or scenario: an earlier value, a limit, or a typical action.
Compare like with like; do not mix a median with a maximum or one request with all requests.

Round only when the task permits a loss of precision. For a limit, financial value,
check result, or technical contract, retain the required precision. If the order of
magnitude matters, add an explanation alongside the original number.

## State an analogy's limits

An analogy transfers a familiar relationship to a new concept. Identify the exact
relationship it explains, then check what other properties a reader might transfer
with it. State the material difference. If caveats outweigh the benefit, use a direct example.

Do not infer system properties from a metaphor. Comparing a job queue with a line
at a checkout helps explain waiting, but does not itself establish execution order,
a single worker, or the absence of retries.

## Story, repetition, and naming

A story helps readers follow a person's choices and their consequences. Preserve
the real participants, chronology, and known causes. Do not turn coincidence into
causation or a complex event into an invented struggle between a hero and a villain.

Repeat an important point when its function changes: a short claim for skimming,
a worked case for understanding, or a reminder before action. Rephrasing without
a new perspective can usually be cut. Do not simulate agreement between independent sources.

A short name for a technique helps readers remember an idea already explained.
Do not invent a term in place of an ordinary word or replace established technical
names with slogans.

## Persuasiveness does not establish truth

Check two things separately: whether the claim is understandable and whether there
is reason to believe it. A vivid story, convenient analogy, confident tone, or polished
chart does not replace evidence. Keep material exceptions even when they complicate the picture.

The skill's teaching examples demonstrate editing techniques; they do not supply facts
about the current task's subject. Check medical, financial, legal, and other specialist
claims against appropriate sources when that verification is part of the task.

## Worked example

Teaching data: 50 exports were checked; 47 finished in less than 10 seconds.
The other three took longer; no data is available for other runs.

Useful wording: “In the check, 47 of 50 exports finished in less than
10 seconds. Three took longer.”

“Exports always take no more than 10 seconds” would be a false promise. Even “94% of
exports finish in under 10 seconds” suggests general statistics if the check is not
identified. To compare versions, first obtain comparable results for the second version.
