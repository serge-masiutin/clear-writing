# Words and sentences

Use after establishing the meaning and the task.

## A warning word is a reason to check the meaning

«Пиши, сокращай» identifies introductory padding, vague expressions, unnecessarily
complex words, unsupported evaluations, and clichés. These are areas to inspect,
not a list of banned words.

| What you find | What to establish | How to edit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “As everyone knows,” “It should be noted that” | Is there a fact here, or only a lead-in? | Start with the fact; support it when needed |
| “May,” “according to estimates,” “approximately” | Is this genuine uncertainty or a verbal habit? | Preserve the limits of knowledge and the precision of the source |
| A complex word | Is the term necessary for precision and familiar to the audience? | Keep the term; explain it nearby if needed |
| “Convenient,” “reliable,” “efficient” | What supports the evaluation, and what does it mean for the reader? | Support it with a fact, scenario, or demonstration; remove it if unsupported |
| “A holistic approach,” “the next level” | What action or property does this stand for? | Name it if known; do not replace one cliché with another |
| “At this point in time” | Does the contrast with an earlier state matter? | Remove empty emphasis on the present; keep a relevant date or period |
| “Very,” “absolutely,” “maximally” | Does the intensifier change a verifiable condition? | Remove empty emphasis; preserve a meaningful limit |

“For example” connects a claim to an example, and “probably” can distinguish a hypothesis
from a fact. Removing them for tidiness can damage the text. A technical “no more than
5 MB” must not become “5 MB”: a limit and a quantity are different claims. Likewise,
do not interchange “must,” “should,” “may,” and “can” when they express different
obligations, recommendations, permissions, or capabilities.

## Evaluations, facts, and usefulness

A fact helps when the reader understands what follows from it. Do not replace an
adjective with impressive but irrelevant numbers. Show how the fact relates to the
reader's task. Without evidence, make the claim more modest or ask for information;
do not add “in 2 seconds,” “without data loss,” or “24/7” to make it persuasive.

Personal opinion belongs in reviews, columns, and personal letters. Preserve its status
as opinion and its source. Do not rewrite “I find this inconvenient” as a proven defect for everyone.

## Actor, action, and circumstances

Find who or what the sentence is about and what happens. If an action is hidden in
a noun, try restoring the verb: “carry out an implementation” → “implement.” Keep
the core close together: who acts, what they do, and what they act on. In English,
avoid separating the subject and main verb with a long series of qualifications.

Active voice helps when the actor is known and matters. If the object's state is the
point or the actor is unknown, do not invent one. “The file was deleted” may be more
accurate than “We deleted the file.” A description of a property need not contain an action.

Check the scope of qualifications. “Users with the admin role can…” must not become
“Users can…”. Move a long condition closer to the action or into its own sentence,
preserving the connection and exceptions.

## Overload and rhythm

Split a sentence when its main subject, time, action, or task changes. Put qualifications,
background, and reasons after the main statement. If a condition determines whether
an action is allowed, state it before the action.

Do not impose a rigid word limit. After splitting, read the sentences together:
are causal connections intact, are pronoun references clear, and does the rhythm
sound monotonous? Sometimes two related short sentences are better joined.

Develop one idea per paragraph. The first sentence helps establish its purpose;
the others add support, detail, or an example. Do not require every paragraph to
have three parts and a separate conclusion: a simple point may need only one sentence.

## Bureaucratic language

A work message usually needs the decision and its consequences first, followed by
the reason and formal basis. Preserve required identifiers, exact legal wording,
and references. If these cannot be edited, improve navigation or add a separate
explanation without replacing the approved text.

## Worked example

Teaching source: “Due to the identification of errors during the review process,
publication of the report cannot be undertaken. The estimated remediation period
is approximately two hours.”

Edit: “The review found errors, so the report cannot be published yet.
The initial estimate is that corrections will take about two hours.”

The reason, current restriction, uncertainty of the estimate, and unit of time remain.
If all you know is that the check failed, do not infer that the report itself contains
errors: first establish what failed the check.
