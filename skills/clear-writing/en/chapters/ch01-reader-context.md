# Reader, context, and interest

Use when a text is formally correct but goes unread, is misunderstood, or meets
with distrust.

## Reader benefit, purpose, and supporting tasks

Separate three questions:

| Anchor | Question | What it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reader benefit | Why would the reader choose to read this? | Audience, topic, and the heading's promise |
| Purpose | What should the reader understand or decide afterwards? | Selection of content |
| Supporting tasks | Which explanations, facts, and examples will get them there? | Outline and missing material |

An author's wish to “announce our move” does not yet establish a benefit. A visitor
needs the new address and directions; an employee needs the date and moving arrangements;
a supplier needs the unloading location. Identify the audience from the request.
If several roles need the document, give them distinct reading paths rather than
making everyone read the same introduction.

Do not invent a reader persona. Treat a known task as a fact and assumptions about
experience or motivation as working hypotheses. Do not turn a small edit into a questionnaire.

## Context before wording

Check who is speaking, where and when the message will appear, and what relationships
and expectations already exist. “Please review the report” reads differently in a reply
to a colleague, an unsolicited email, and a notification during an outage.

- If the reader needs to solve an urgent problem, start with the action or status they need.
- If expectations may differ from the content, state the purpose and limits up front:
  whether this is an overview or instructions, what knowledge is needed, and what remains unverified.
- If there is a known reason for distrust, acknowledge the specific fact and its effects.
  Do not attribute thoughts to the reader, such as “You must be furious.”
- If the message does not reach its recipient or the product does not deliver its promise,
  identify the obstacle. Editing cannot confirm delivery or fix the product.

Do not transfer a format between channels automatically: a marketing teaser gets in
the way of an error message; a detailed history of preparation gets in the way of a results report.

## Three kinds of reader benefit

«Ясно, понятно» distinguishes practical, social, and emotional benefits.

- **Practical:** solve a problem, choose, or save effort. Start with a recognizable
  situation. A name for the problem is useless if the reader does not yet know that name.
- **Social:** find one's way in a professional community, share something useful,
  or understand others. Show a real opportunity; do not promise status from a purchase.
- **Emotional:** find enjoyment, interest, or relief. Use this when it suits the format
  and the reader's situation; do not replace substantive value with clickbait.

For work instructions and UI, a practical benefit is usually enough. Every message
does not need suspense, a joke, or an appeal to the reader's self-esteem.

## Prior knowledge and depth

Adapt the explanation to what the reader knows. A beginner needs a first step and
an explanation of the concept; a specialist needs distinctions, conditions of use,
and consequences. Depth comes from answering real questions, examples, limits, and
difficulties, not from the number of technical terms.

If advice seems obvious, check what makes it difficult to follow. Instead of explaining
once more why being organized is useful, show what to do about a specific obstacle.
Do not invent an unusual rule merely because someone already knows the correct one.

## Speak as an equal

Describe the problem and the available next step, not a judgment of the person. Do not
expect readers to know what seems obvious to the author. Preserve the requested voice
as long as it serves the task; a neutral tone is a starting point for work communication,
not a ban on emotion.

Care shows in useful action: an exact link, a clear condition, or an explanation of
the next step. “We care about you” is not enough on its own. Editing the text does
not authorize taking that step or contacting the recipient.

## Worked example

Teaching scenario: an export failed to create a file; the cause is not yet known.
The user can run the export again.

- Weak: “You exported the file incorrectly. Please pay closer attention.”
- Useful: “The file could not be created. Try again.”

The focus has changed: the user sees the outcome and an available action. We have
not invented a cause, blamed the person, or promised that another attempt will succeed.
