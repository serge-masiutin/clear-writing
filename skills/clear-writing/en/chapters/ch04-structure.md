# Structure and navigation

Use when the material is available but difficult to read or search for an answer.

## Choose a reading path

| What the reader is doing | Suitable order | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Learning about an event or status | Result → consequences → details → background | The author's work history before the result |
| Following instructions | Conditions → preparation → steps → result check | Revealing prerequisites after a step that depends on them |
| Choosing between options | Criteria → comparable options → limitations | Different criteria chosen to flatter each option |
| Understanding how something works | Overview → parts and relationships → a specific case | Terms explained only after they are used |
| Evaluating a conclusion | Claim → support → limits of the conclusion | Treating an example as proof or hiding a premise |
| Following a story | Situation and person → task → actions → outcome | Irrelevant details or invented motives and causes |

Structures can be combined. For example, a report can start with the result and
put the chronology in a separate section. Choose this to suit the task, not to use every form.

## Two levels for a complex document

If a specialist needs the details but a busy decision-maker needs the conclusion,
retain the detail and add a short entry point: status, conclusion, consequences,
and the decision required. After reading it, the reader should know whether action is needed.

The short layer must not contradict the full one: a qualification that changes a
decision belongs in the summary too. Technical identifiers and evidence can remain
below. Do not duplicate complete instructions across documents; link to the main source.

## Paragraphs and headings

Check that each paragraph develops a distinct idea rather than filling a convenient
number of lines. A subheading helps readers choose a section and anticipate its
content. A short message may not need separate headings.

Read only the headings and first sentences of paragraphs. Are the topic, order, and
main point apparent? Then read everything: are connections intact, and are there
logical leaps? In a story or a sequential argument, do not demand that every paragraph
stand alone; independence matters chiefly for material intended for selective reading.

Use similarly structured sections to review comparable items. Preserve the sequence
when an argument depends on earlier points. Do not turn a chain of causes into a
list of independent facts.

## List, table, or paragraph

- Use a list for comparable options, features, or short independent points.
- Use numbering when the order of actions or references to steps matter.
- Use a table to compare several items using the same parameters.
- Keep a connected explanation of cause and effect in prose.
- If each item needs its own long explanation, make them sections.

Start an item with what the reader searches for: “If the email hasn't arrived…” is
more useful in instructions than an internal subsystem name. Add a deep hierarchy
only when necessary; do not require readers to remember several levels for a short text.

## Opening and ending

An opening provides orientation: who the material helps, what it contains, and what
conditions apply. Remove an introduction that merely announces the topic's importance
or repeats the heading.

An ending should serve a remaining need: a next step, a decision criterion, or a material
limitation. If everything has been said, do not add a ceremonial conclusion. A short
checklist can help at the end of a long teaching document when it supports application.

## Worked example

Teaching source: “Upload the archive. It must be signed before uploading.
The signature is created with a key available only to the administrator.”

Edit: “Uploading requires an archive signed by the administrator. First, the
administrator signs the archive with their key. Then upload the signed archive.”

The access condition and preparation now precede the action. The order has changed,
but permission to sign has not been broadened. Do not add a signing command absent from the source.
