# Editing patterns

Choose one technique for the problem you have found. The examples here are hypothetical;
they adapt the two books' methods, rather than describe product behavior.

## Cut clutter and add substance

**When:** a sentence sounds impressive but says nothing useful.

1. Find the claim beneath the evaluations and bureaucratic phrasing.
2. Remove what neither changes the meaning nor helps the reading.
3. Check whether what remains is enough for the reader's task.
4. Add a verified property, condition, or scenario if one is missing.
5. Split the sentence if the facts overload it.

**Limit:** do not add invented data to fill out the text. Sometimes the result is
a modest, accurate statement and an identified gap in the information.

## Claim, example, and limits of application

**When:** correct advice could be interpreted too broadly or used incorrectly.

1. State the advice for a specific situation.
2. Show one action and its result.
3. Add a likely mistake if it helps distinguish the cases.
4. Explain the condition that makes the decisions different.

**Limit:** do not expand this structure for an obvious action. One case does not
establish an event's frequency or prove a universal rule.

## “For the reader, this means”

**When:** the text is full of product features, awards, or internal achievements.

1. Find a verified property.
2. Establish which action or limitation it changes for the reader.
3. Check the causal link; remove consequences that do not follow from the property.
4. Present the property with its useful consequence, if one exists.

**Limit:** “servers in two regions” does not by itself mean “data cannot be lost.”
If the real consequence is unknown, keep the original fact without a promise.

## Two layers for a complex document

**When:** the reader needs a quick conclusion, but details must not be removed.

1. Group the source material by the reader's questions.
2. Put each group's main point at its beginning.
3. Bring a short status and the required decision to the top.
4. Keep evidence, conditions, and background below.
5. Compare the short layer with the full one: no important exception should disappear.

**Limit:** a summary does not replace mandatory text. If editing is prohibited,
add only the explanation or formatting that is permitted.

## Check by skimming

**When:** instructions, a report, or a page are designed for finding a relevant passage.

1. Read the headings and paragraph openings without the body text.
2. Check whether readers can find their question and understand the main conclusion.
3. Look only at the illustrations and captions: do they imply the same conclusion?
4. Read the full text, checking connections and conditions.

**Limit:** skimming does not replace full reading for a connected story or proof.
Do not break a logical sequence to make each fragment stand alone.
